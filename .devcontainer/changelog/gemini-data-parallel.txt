What should I do for operations that aren't easily parallelizable? Should they remain single threaded?


Yes, operations that aren't easily parallelizable or have strong sequential dependencies should generally remain single-threaded.

The "Primary Node" Strategy
For these types of operations, the most effective strategy is to designate a "primary" or "main" NUMA node (often node 0) to handle them. When your dispatcher encounters an operation like GGML_OP_RESHAPE or GGML_OP_DIAG_MASK_INF (which have complex data dependencies or low computational intensity), it should simply schedule it to run on a single thread on the primary node.

This approach has two main benefits:

Avoids Overhead: It prevents the unnecessary overhead of dispatching, managing, and synchronizing multiple threads for a task that won't benefit from it. The cost of waking up and coordinating threads can easily exceed the time it takes to just do the work on one thread.

Prevents Synchronization Hell: It sidesteps complex synchronization problems. For operations where the result of one step is required for the very next step, forcing parallelism can lead to massive wait times (lock contention) as threads wait for each other, effectively serializing the process anyway but with added overhead.

Amdahl's Law in Action
This is a practical application of Amdahl's Law, a principle in parallel computing. It states that the maximum speedup you can achieve is limited by the portion of the program that must be executed sequentially.

By running parallelizable operations like GEMM and RoPE across all your NUMA nodes and running sequential operations on a single, dedicated node, you are optimizing both parts of the equation. You're getting the maximum speedup where possible and minimizing overhead where it's not. Your overall performance will be a balance between these two components.

Excellent question. You've correctly identified the next critical design decision. While a compute graph is what your dispatcher will analyze, it's too high-level to be the "work item" you enqueue for the coordinator.

The best abstraction is a simple but powerful work item struct that bundles a function pointer with its specific data payload. This is a highly flexible and common pattern in task-based parallelism.

The Work Item Abstraction
Think of the work item as a sealed envelope. The dispatcher's job is to prepare the envelope, and the coordinator's job is to open it and follow the instructions inside without needing to understand the bigger picture.

Here's what the abstraction would look like in C:

C

// A generic function pointer for any task our workers can execute.
// It takes a pointer to its own data payload.
typedef void (*ggml_task_t)(void *task_data);

// The "envelope" that the dispatcher sends to the coordinator.
// It's small, generic, and flexible.
typedef struct {
    ggml_task_t func;  // The function to execute (the "instructions").
    void * data;  // A pointer to the specific data for this task (the "contents").
} ggml_work_item_t;
With this, your coordinator becomes incredibly simple. Each worker thread in its pool just does this:

Dequeue a ggml_work_item_t.

Execute item.func(item.data).

The intelligence is now fully contained within the dispatcher, which is exactly where it belongs.
